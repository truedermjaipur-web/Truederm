Vitiligo (Safed Daag): Causes, Common Myths and Treatment Options Explained

By Dr. Iftekhar Khan, MBBS, MD Dermatology (AIIMS Delhi) · Truederm, C-Scheme, Jaipur · 6 min read
Consultation at Truederm, C-Scheme, Jaipur

Noticing a white patch on your skin, or your child's, can be worrying. Families often arrive at the clinic with a long list of foods they have already stopped, home remedies they have tried, and fears about whether it will spread to others. This guide explains what vitiligo is, what it isn't, and how it is treated today.

What is vitiligo?

Vitiligo (also called leucoderma, or safed daag in Hindi) is a condition in which the skin loses its pigment-producing cells, called melanocytes. Without them, the skin turns milky white in patches. It is widely understood to be an autoimmune condition, where the body's immune system targets its own pigment cells, and it can run in families. It can start at any age and can appear anywhere, commonly on the face, hands, feet, lips, and around the eyes and joints.

Myths that need to go

  • "It is contagious." No. Vitiligo does not spread through touch, food, sharing utensils or living together.
  • "It's caused by eating fish with milk, or sour foods." There is no scientific evidence that any food causes vitiligo. Unnecessary food restrictions, especially in children, can do more harm than good.
  • "It is related to leprosy." No. They are completely different conditions.
  • "Nothing can be done." Not true. Many patients regain colour with treatment, especially when it starts early.
Not every white patch is vitiligo. Fungal infections, pityriasis alba (common in children), post-inflammatory marks and some other conditions can also cause light or white patches, and they are treated very differently. A dermatologist's examination, sometimes with a Wood's lamp, is the first step.

Stable vs spreading vitiligo

Treatment depends a great deal on whether the vitiligo is active (new patches appearing or existing ones growing) or stable (no change for a long period). The first goal in active vitiligo is to stop the spread. Once it is stable, the focus shifts to bringing colour back.

Treatment options

  • Creams and ointments that calm the immune reaction in the skin, chosen based on the site (the face and eyelids need different care from the hands and feet)
  • Oral medicines in selected cases to control rapidly spreading vitiligo, prescribed and monitored by the dermatologist
  • Phototherapy (light treatment), such as narrowband UVB, which encourages pigment cells to return; it usually needs regular sessions over several months
  • Surgical options such as skin or cell grafting, considered for patches that have been stable for a long time and haven't responded to medicines
  • Camouflage and sun protection; white patches burn easily, so sunscreen is important

Re-pigmentation is gradual. Small dots of colour often appear first, especially around hair follicles, and spread slowly over months. The face and neck usually respond better than the hands, feet and lips. Regular follow-up lets the doctor adjust treatment as the skin responds.

Living with vitiligo

Vitiligo is a medical condition, not a reflection of health, hygiene or character. For many people, especially teenagers and young adults, the emotional impact is real. Talking openly with family, and with your doctor about treatment goals, helps. Treatment is a partnership, and steady follow-up matters more than any single miracle remedy.

When to see a dermatologist

See a dermatologist as soon as you notice a new white patch, if patches are spreading, or if a child develops light patches. Early diagnosis means the right treatment can begin sooner.

Frequently asked questions

Can vitiligo be cured completely?

Vitiligo can often be controlled, and many patches regain colour with treatment, especially on the face and when treatment starts early. Response varies from person to person, so your dermatologist will set realistic goals with you.

Does eating certain foods cause vitiligo?

No. There is no scientific evidence that foods such as fish with milk or sour foods cause vitiligo or make it spread.

Is vitiligo contagious?

No. Vitiligo does not spread from person to person through touch, food or sharing utensils.
